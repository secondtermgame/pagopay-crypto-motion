# Crypto-to-Fiat Conversion: How It Works and What It Costs

Every time you spend crypto with a card, a quiet conversion happens in the background: digital assets become spendable USD. That moment is where most of the cost — and most of the hidden fees — lives.

This guide explains how **crypto-to-fiat conversion** works, the difference between fair pricing and hidden spreads, and how to **convert crypto to cash** without losing more than you should.

## What "Conversion" Really Means

When you fund a crypto card balance, your BTC, ETH, USDC, or USDT is exchanged for US dollars at a market rate. From that point on, the card balance behaves like a normal USD account.

There are two common models:

- **Convert on deposit** — Crypto becomes USD the moment you fund the card. Your spend price is locked in.
- **Convert on spend** — Crypto stays as crypto until each purchase, then converts in real time.

Each has trade-offs. Convert-on-deposit removes price risk between funding and spending. Convert-on-spend keeps you exposed to the market until you actually pay.

## How the Price Is Set

A fair conversion uses live market rates from major exchanges, plus a transparent fee. The total cost you pay is typically made of:

1. **Market rate** — The mid-market price for BTC, ETH, or another asset at the moment of conversion.
2. **Conversion fee** — A clearly disclosed percentage charged on the conversion.
3. **FX fee (if any)** — Charged when the resulting USD is spent in a different currency.

Together these determine your real cost of moving from **bitcoin to fiat** or **ethereum to USD**.

## Where Hidden Costs Hide

"Zero fees" sounds great until you look closely. The most common hidden cost is the **spread**: the gap between the market rate and the rate the provider actually uses. A provider can advertise no fee while quietly applying a 2–3% spread.

A transparent provider will:

- Show you the live market rate.
- Show the conversion fee as a clearly labeled line item.
- Charge a consistent rate rather than a moving spread.

## A Concrete Example

Suppose you convert $1,000 worth of BTC into your card balance with a 3% conversion fee:

- $1,000 in BTC at market rate → ~$970 of spendable USD on your card.
- The remaining $30 is the conversion fee.

Now contrast that with a card that hides a 5% spread but advertises "no fees":

- $1,000 in BTC at market rate → ~$950 of spendable USD on your card.
- No itemized "fee," but $50 quietly disappeared.

The "no fees" card is more expensive in practice.

## Stablecoins Change the Math

If you fund with **stablecoins** like USDC or USDT, the math is much simpler — you're not converting BTC or ETH into USD, just moving USD-backed assets into a USD card balance. You still pay any disclosed funding fee, but you avoid price volatility entirely. Read more in [Why Stablecoins Are Changing Everyday Spending](/blog/stablecoins-everyday-spending).

## FX on Top of Conversion

If you spend in a currency other than USD, an additional **FX fee** can apply. This is separate from the crypto conversion and worth checking carefully:

- Some cards charge 1–2% on every international transaction.
- A few are zero-FX, which can save real money for travelers.

For the full international picture, see [How to Travel with a Crypto Card in 2026](/blog/crypto-card-travel-guide).

## How to Evaluate a Crypto Card's Fees

When comparing cards, ignore marketing language and check four things:

1. **Disclosed conversion fee** — Look for a clearly stated percentage.
2. **Spread vs market rate** — Test by converting a small amount and comparing to a live rate.
3. **FX fee** — Critical for anyone spending internationally.
4. **Card and monthly fees** — Sometimes a higher-tier card with no monthly fee is cheaper overall.
